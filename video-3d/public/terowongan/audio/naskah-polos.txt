You're a well digger, and you've just been offered $10 billion to build a road under the sea. 20 miles long. But you only get paid if it's finished. The problem is, you've never dug anything wider than a well. But you really want a jet-powered sports car. So here's how you get the money!
First, you grab a shovel. But one shovel would take your whole life. So you build a giant spinning drill, with steel teeth, that grinds rock into mud. This is called a tunnel boring machine.
But the second you dig, the walls fall in. So a robot arm bolts concrete blocks into a ring behind the drill. Ring after ring. This is called a segment lining.
But 300 feet under the sea, water presses on every inch. So you pump liquid concrete behind every ring, and seal it tight. This is called grouting.
Then, halfway under the sea, your drill hits a crack in the rock. A crack full of seawater. It blasts in. Thousands of gallons a minute! You pump. You patch. It keeps coming. If the tunnel floods, you lose the machine, the tunnel, and every single dollar.
So you go home. You sit against the very first well you ever dug, and you think about quitting.
And then, you remember. When you drilled this well, water burst in too. So you poured in thick clay mud, heavier than water. It pushed the water back, and stuck to the walls like plaster.
So you go back down. You seal the front of the drill, and fill it with that same clay mud, pumped in hard. It pushes the sea back, and plugs the crack. This is called a slurry shield. The tunnel under Tokyo Bay was dug with machines just like it.
But now you're months behind. So you dig from both countries at once, toward each other, in the dark. Miss by a few feet, and you've built two very expensive holes. So lasers guide both machines.
And they meet. Less than 2 feet apart! This is called the breakthrough.
You get your $10 billion, and you buy the jet-powered sports car. Just one problem: your giant drill can't back out. So you bury it under the sea. It's still down there.
